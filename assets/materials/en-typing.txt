Setup and posture

Sit upright with your back supported and both feet flat on the floor.
Keep your elbows close to your body and bent at about ninety degrees.
Adjust the chair height so your forearms are level with the keyboard.
Keep your wrists straight, not resting on the desk or the wrist pad.
Let your shoulders drop; tension there travels straight into your fingers.
Place the screen at eye level so you do not tilt your head down.
Keep the screen about an arm's length away from your eyes.
Use a light touch; the keys need far less force than most people apply.
Dim the room slightly so the screen is the brightest thing you look at.
Keep your desk clear so your hands have room to move.
Put the keyboard flat or with a very slight negative tilt.
Learn where the Enter, Shift, Backspace, and Tab keys sit by feel.
Do not look at the keyboard while you type; look at the screen.
Say the letters in your head at first, then let the words take over.
Warm up your fingers before a long session with a few easy lines.
Cold hands make more mistakes, so wash them in warm water if needed.
Keep a glass of water nearby and sip between exercises.
Turn off notifications while you practice; interruptions break rhythm.
Practice in a quiet place until the movements become automatic.
Use the same keyboard every day while you are still learning.
A mechanical keyboard is not required; consistency matters more.
Set the repeat delay long enough that a held key does not flood the line.
Check your keyboard layout once, then trust it.
If the layout differs from the labels, print a small map and tape it down.
Keep the mouse close so your right hand does not travel far.
Use keyboard shortcuts instead of the mouse whenever you can.
Learn to press Shift with the opposite hand from the letter you type.
Use the right Shift for letters typed with the left hand, and the reverse.
Use Caps Lock only for long runs of capitals, never for single letters.
Enter should be pressed with the right little finger, not the whole hand.

The home row and your fingers

Rest your fingers on the home row: a, s, d, f and j, k, l, semicolon.
Feel the small bumps on the f and j keys; they mark your index fingers.
Left little finger owns q, a, z, and the keys to their left.
Left ring finger owns w, s, and x.
Left middle finger owns e, d, and c.
Left index finger owns r, t, f, g, v, and b.
Right index finger owns y, u, h, j, n, and m.
Right middle finger owns i, k, and the comma.
Right ring finger owns o, l, and the period.
Right little finger owns p, semicolon, slash, and the keys to their right.
The thumbs share the space bar; use the one that feels natural.
Move only the finger you need, not the whole hand.
After every reach, return that finger to its home key.
Keep the other fingers resting while one finger reaches out.
Reach upward for the top row and downward for the bottom row.
Do not slide your hand sideways to reach a key.
The number row belongs to the same fingers as the letters below it.
For 1 and 2 use the left little finger and left ring finger.
For 8, 9, and 0 use the right middle, ring, and little fingers.
Treat the shifted symbols as the same key: ! lives on 1, @ on 2, and so on.
The apostrophe and the semicolon share the right little finger.
Brackets, braces, and pipes all live under the right little finger.
Backspace is a right little finger key, not a hand movement.
Tab is a left little finger reach, and Escape is a left little finger stretch.
Keep your index fingers anchored until a reach is genuinely needed.
When you type a space, let the thumb move alone.
Practice the home row until you can find f and j with your eyes closed.
Type full words instead of single letters once the row feels familiar.
Alternate hands as much as possible; it keeps the rhythm even.
Avoid crossing fingers over each other for awkward combinations.
If a key is hard to reach, check your hand position instead of forcing it.

Accuracy before speed

Accuracy is the habit; speed is the result of repeating it.
Type slowly enough that you can hit the right key every time.
A wrong character costs far more time than a slow correct one.
When you feel yourself speeding up past control, slow down at once.
Correct mistakes immediately, then continue without dwelling on them.
Do not delete an entire line because of one error.
Read the text one word ahead of the keys you are pressing.
Look at whole words, not single characters.
Let capital letters and punctuation be part of the word you see.
If you keep missing one key, stop and drill that key alone.
Check whether the mistake comes from the finger or from your eyes.
Most mistakes come from looking away or from moving the whole hand.
Keep your eyes on the text and let your fingers do the finding.
Accuracy at 30 words per minute beats flailing at 80.
Never trade correctness for the feeling of speed.
If your error rate rises above a few percent, slow down.
Finish the line even if it is slow; stopping breaks the rhythm.
The last three words of a line deserve as much care as the first.
Do not guess words ahead of what the text actually says.
When you backspace, retype the whole word, not just one letter.
Watch for doubled letters and missing letters in common words.
Slow down before punctuation, then resume your pace.
Keep your movements small and your presses crisp.
Rest your eyes on the line as if you were reading, not copying.
Accuracy improves fastest in short, focused sessions.
Correct posture and light pressure reduce accidental key presses.

Rhythm and speed

Think of typing as music: steady beats beat sudden bursts.
Find a rhythm you can hold for the whole exercise.
Speed grows from removing wasted motion, not from pressing harder.
Keep both hands moving; a still hand is wasted time.
Let the next key's finger start moving before you finish the current one.
Aim for a smooth stream of characters rather than individual keystrokes.
Do not pause after finishing a word; move straight into the next.
Breathe evenly and keep your shoulders still while you type.
Relax the ring and little fingers; they tense up first.
When you feel tense, shake out your hands and start again slowly.
Increase your speed only after accuracy at the current pace is stable.
Push the pace for short bursts, then return to your normal rhythm.
A metronome or a steady beat in your head can help.
Count in groups of four or five characters to keep a pulse.
Fast typing looks calm; frantic typing looks busy and produces errors.
Use the strength of each finger: index fingers do the most work.
Give the weaker fingers more practice, not less.
Repeat the same tricky word ten times rather than the whole line once.
Speed comes from knowing where the key is, not from looking for it.
Trust your fingers; hesitation costs more than a wrong key.
Keep the same rhythm across punctuation and capitals.
Slow is smooth, smooth is fast; give the habits time to settle.
Record your best speed and try to hold it comfortably, not just once.
Stop a speed drill while you still feel in control.
Chase consistency first, then raise the ceiling.

Habits to avoid

Do not rest your wrists on the desk while your fingers are typing.
Do not look down to find a key; that habit undoes weeks of practice.
Do not move your whole hand for a key your finger can reach.
Do not hammer the keys; pressure does not create speed.
Do not hold your breath while concentrating.
Do not let one weak finger force the others out of position.
Do not chase a personal best on every session.
Do not practice only what you are already good at.
Do not skip the home row because it feels slow.
Do not ignore a repeated mistake; name it and drill it.
Do not type through pain in your wrists or forearms.
Do not keep a death grip on the mouse between sentences.
Do not slouch when you get tired; take a break instead.
Do not compare your speed with anyone else's.
Do not practice for two hours once a week.
Do not use the same three lines for every session.
Do not let the cursor run ahead of your eyes.
Do not delete the whole paragraph to fix a single typo.
Do not type with two fingers and hope for improvement.
Do not skip the easy drills; they are the foundation.

A practice plan that works

Practice a little every day; fifteen minutes beats two hours on Sunday.
Split your session into a warm-up, a main drill, and a cool-down.
Warm up with the home row and easy words.
Spend most of the session on your current weak spot.
Finish with something easy so you end on a good note.
Review your error keys at the end of each session.
Write down the three keys that caused the most trouble.
Start the next session by drilling those three keys.
Change the material often enough to stay interested.
Revisit old lessons; they should feel easier each time.
Use timed tests to measure, not to learn.
Take a full day off when your hands feel tired.
Track your speed weekly, not after every line.
Measure accuracy together with speed; one without the other is noise.
Set a goal for the month, not for the day.
Increase difficulty when your accuracy stays above ninety-five percent.
Keep a short log of what felt hard and what felt easy.
Alternate between text you enjoy and text you need.
Practice punctuation and capitals as their own drill.
Practice numbers as their own drill; they use longer reaches.
Practice with a metronome once a week to check your rhythm.
Review your posture in a mirror or with a photo now and then.
Ask someone to watch your hands and point out wasted motion.
Reward yourself for consistency, not for a single fast run.
When progress stalls, change the drill, not the goal.
A plateau usually means your accuracy is quietly improving.
Retyping a text you know well shows whether the skill is automatic.
Type a paragraph aloud from memory as a fun check.
Teach the home row to someone else; it sharpens your own habit.

Drills worth repeating

Type the home row keys in order, then backward, ten times.
Type each letter of the alphabet three times without looking.
Type the alphabet in pairs: ab, cd, ef, and so on.
Type common two-letter combinations from the home row.
Drill the left hand alone for one line, then the right hand.
Type a line using only the index fingers, then only the middle fingers.
Practice the four hardest reaches for your little fingers.
Type a paragraph of plain lowercase words with no punctuation.
Type the same paragraph with correct capitals and punctuation.
Type a paragraph of numbers and symbols only.
Type a paragraph with alternating hands, like "the cat sat".
Drill doubled letters: ss, ll, ee, oo, tt, ff.
Drill common endings: ing, tion, ment, ness, ly, ed.
Drill common beginnings: th, wh, sh, ch, pr, tr, str.
Type the same sentence with your eyes closed between words.
Type with a slight delay after each mistake to reset your rhythm.
Read a sentence once, then type it from memory.
Type a sentence while counting out loud to break the hesitation habit.
Use a short word list of your most frequent errors.
Type a full page without looking at the keyboard, however slow.
Time yourself on the same paragraph once a week.
Compare the timed run with last week's, not with anyone else's.
Drill the space bar by typing alternating single letters.
Drill the Enter key by typing a list of short lines.
Drill Tab and Shift by typing a small table.
Type a paragraph of dialogue to practice quotation marks.
Type a paragraph with many commas and semicolons.
Type a paragraph of all caps, then one of mixed case.
Type a short paragraph backward, one word at a time, for control.
Finish every drill by retyping your worst line correctly.

Health and comfort

Take a short break every twenty to thirty minutes.
Stand up and stretch your forearms and shoulders during the break.
Look at something far away for twenty seconds to rest your eyes.
Relax your grip; sore fingers usually mean too much force.
Keep your hands warm; cold fingers make more mistakes.
Stretch your fingers gently before and after practice.
If your wrists ache, stop and rest for a day or two.
Adjust the keyboard height rather than bending your wrists.
Avoid resting your elbows on hard armrests for long periods.
Blink often; staring at the screen dries your eyes.
Keep the screen clean so you do not lean in to read it.
Use a document holder when typing from paper.
Keep the room at a comfortable temperature.
Wear glasses if you need them; squinting adds tension.
Do not type through numbness or tingling in your hands.
Move your whole arm when reaching for the mouse, not just the wrist.
Keep the mouse at the same height as the keyboard.
Reduce glare from windows behind or in front of the screen.
Sit far enough back that your elbows stay loose.
Change your sitting position slightly every few minutes.

Mindset and progress

Speed is a byproduct of accuracy and relaxation, not of effort.
Every expert typist was once slow and clumsy.
Mistakes are information about which key to practice next.
A bad session is still practice; do not throw it away.
Progress is not linear; plateaus are normal and temporary.
Compare yourself with last month, not with last year.
Celebrate small wins: one more accurate line is a win.
Keep the sessions short enough that you want to return tomorrow.
If you dread practice, make the material easier or shorter.
Type things you find interesting; interest sustains practice.
Do not treat a slow day as proof that you cannot improve.
Rest is part of training, not a break from it.
Consistency beats intensity over weeks and months.
When you feel frustrated, slow down instead of pushing harder.
Finish each session knowing one thing you did well.
Learn from a wrong key instead of apologizing for it.
Trust the process even when the numbers move slowly.
Teach your hands patience; they learn by repetition, not by pressure.

Beyond the basics

Learn the shortcuts for your editor and browser to reduce mouse use.
Use both hands for shortcuts: one for the modifier, one for the key.
Learn to touch type numbers without leaving the home row.
Learn the shifted symbols as their own small vocabulary.
Practice code or markup if you write it; brackets need their own drill.
Practice writing in your second language; the letter patterns differ.
Practice typing from dictation once in a while.
Practice reading ahead in longer texts.
Type a page of a book every day until it becomes boring.
When it becomes boring, your hands have learned it.
Move on to a new text and keep the old one for warm-ups.
Test yourself on unfamiliar material, not just the text you rehearsed.
Type on a laptop keyboard now and then; the feel is different.
Learn where the awkward keys are on each keyboard you use.
Keep a small error log and review it weekly.
Aim to make the keyboard disappear from your attention.
When you stop thinking about the keys, you are done learning the basics.
Everything after that is refinement.

Typing in the real world

Write a short email without correcting anything until the end.
Draft a note to yourself in full sentences instead of fragments.
Type a shopping list with numbers and units.
Type a recipe and pay attention to the numbers and the order.
Type a schedule with times, dates, and weekday names.
Type an address and a phone number from memory.
Type a paragraph from a news article and keep the punctuation exact.
Type a message you would normally send on your phone.
Type a short poem and let the line breaks be part of the rhythm.
Type a page of dialogue and keep the quotation marks straight.
Type a table using Tab between the columns.
Type a list using Enter at the end of each line.
Type a paragraph in all caps, then convert it to sentence case in your head.
Type a URL and then delete it, letter by letter.
Type a file path with slashes, dots, and dashes.
Type a short command line and check every symbol.
Type a few lines of code and match the indentation.
Type a paragraph of mixed Chinese and English if you use both.
Type a sentence with every kind of bracket in it.
Type a sentence with three commas and no full stop, then add one.
Type a question and answer pair as fast as you can read them.
Type a paragraph while a timer runs, then stop at exactly one minute.
Type the same paragraph again and try to beat your accuracy, not your speed.
Type a paragraph from a page you have not read before.
Type a paragraph from a book you know by heart and notice the old habits.
Type a line of lyrics and keep the capitals where the title has them.
Type a line of dialogue twice: once plainly, once with emphasis.
Type a short paragraph and read it back to yourself for errors.
Type an error message exactly as it appears on screen.
Type a version number and a date without looking at the keyboard.
Type a sentence that contains every vowel.
Type a sentence that contains no repeated letters if you can.
Type the alphabet backward once a day; it exposes weak fingers.
Type your own name and address until they are automatic.
Type a short paragraph with your eyes closed between words.
Type a paragraph and correct every mistake twice before moving on.
